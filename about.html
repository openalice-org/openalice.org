About OpenAlice

Universities, companies and research programmes are easy to find. Who actually works with whom is not. OpenAlice is a map of the people who do research and of how they are connected: start with a person and see who they work with, what they work on, and how that has changed over time.

The first map is Quantum New Nordics: the people working in quantum technology across the region. It was sponsored by the Quantum Sweden Innovation Platform (QSIP) and built with the KTH Library’s bibliometrics team, who advised us through the whole process.

Where the data comes from

Everything on the map comes from public sources. OpenAlex supplies papers, affiliations and research topics. Semantic Scholar and the Open Academic Graph add publications and citations. Patents come from the European Patent Office, and each university’s own web pages fill in the rest.

What counts as quantum

A paper is on the map when its topics and keywords, and where needed its own title or abstract, show that quantum physics is central to it rather than merely mentioned. Every paper records the rule that put it there. When the evidence is not enough, the paper is left out rather than guessed.

People are harder, because one researcher can appear under several records. We merge two records only when they share real coauthors. The map is approximate by design: good enough to find your way by, not a register of who anyone is.

Where it comes from

OpenAlice grew out of Prenovation, an outreach experiment at KTH Innovation. It began in QSIP, where we tried knocking first: going out to research environments before an idea reached our door, instead of waiting for it. To know where to knock we needed a map of who was working on what. Alice turns that way of working into a tool others can use.

Code and data

The code is open source under the MIT License, and the source is on GitHub. The data is not part of the code. Each map is a separate shard, governed by the sources it was built from.
